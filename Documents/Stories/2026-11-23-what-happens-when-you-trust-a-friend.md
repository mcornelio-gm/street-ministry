Over time, several of the folks I met on the streets have become genuine personal friends--people I hang out with and check in on outside of Tuesday nights.

One of my favorite ongoing stories started a few years ago when a former teammate on our route reached out to me. She had volunteered with us for a long time before stepping back for personal reasons. Her mother-in-law had suffered a stroke, and her family was looking for a live-in caretaker. What surprised me was how adamant she was about hiring one of our street friends for the role.

Honestly, at first, I told her I didn't think it was a good idea. They were specifically looking for a woman, and there simply weren't many women out on our route. The one woman they kept focusing on wanted to help, but she kept making promises she just couldn't keep.

I had someone else in mind--a guy I knew personally from the route--but since he didn't check the box of being female, he didn't fit what they were originally looking for. Still, after running into dead ends, the family finally agreed to at least sit down and have a conversation with him. I vouched for him because I knew his character firsthand, and I promised I'd stay in close touch, checking in on him regularly to encourage him and make sure everything was going smoothly.

That conversation changed everything. It takes a tremendous amount of courage--and real faith--to welcome someone you met on the streets into your home to care for a vulnerable family member.

I caught up with him just a couple of weeks ago, and he told me he is celebrating his fourth anniversary as her caretaker. Not only is he still faithfully caring for her after four years, but he has also landed a full-time job on top of it.

So often, our friends on the streets are locked out of life-changing opportunities simply because they don't have a permanent address or a traditional resume. When someone takes a chance on them and they rise to the occasion, it becomes a powerful testimony. Those of us in street ministry get to witness their resilience and goodness every week--my hope is that stories like his help everyone else see what we see: our street friends are just like us, simply walking through a different season of life.
